# What the Numbers Actually Measure

**As of: 2026-10-13**

A coding agent finishes its task, and the bill is lower. Is that enough to give it more work? For a small team, the answer depends on what the task measured—and what happened after the agent stopped. The supplied evidence supports active, bounded trials of coding assistants, but not a general claim that they make small teams deliver software faster.

Asana reports a 144-run study in which four models handled the same browser task: collect six fields for each of 32 books from a public demo catalog. Its optimized GPT-6.1 Sol workflow averaged about four minutes and $0.47 in estimated model costs; Asana compares it with the original production setup on Model B as five times faster and 76 times cheaper. Asana also says every optimized run completed the task with the correct answer. [02-c1] [02-c2]

That is a useful result for the workflow tested. It makes a positive case for experimentation: a team can examine a repeatable task, vary settings, and check whether quality holds while runtime and estimated model expense fall. But the comparison does not count total delivery effort, including setup, review, rework, or maintenance, and a fixed catalog task does not establish performance across varied software work. The account is published by OpenAI, which has a commercial interest in the models and tools described; that context matters when interpreting the result. [02-c1] [02-c2]

ReviewBench, a benchmark described by GitHub, asks a different question: how well do code-review agents find and report issues? GitHub says the benchmark uses representative pull requests, multiple sources of ground truth, and six metrics. It reports that senior engineers who independently relabeled findings agreed with its judgments 96.6% of the time. GitHub also says ReviewBench’s offline results pointed in the same direction as a later Copilot code-review A/B test. These details describe a validation approach and a reported relationship between benchmark and production results; they are not an independent estimate of productivity for small teams. [02-c4]

The distinction matters because task completion, review quality, and delivery efficiency are related but separate outcomes. Asana’s browser runs record correctness, time, and estimated model cost for one fixed task. ReviewBench evaluates code-review findings against benchmark judgments. Their results cannot be combined into a general estimate of faster delivery: the tasks, measures, and conditions do not match. That conclusion is an inference from the different evaluation targets, not a claim either source makes. [02-c4]

A third signal concerns protection as software activity scales. GitHub reports that between Q2 2024 and Q2 2026, screened pushes grew 2.84 times while pushes carrying credentials grew 2.59 times, with no statistically detectable trend in per-push prevalence. The figures describe GitHub’s reported secret-protection trend; they do not measure coding-agent productivity or show what caused the trend. GitHub sells security products, so its account is useful evidence about the measures it tracks, not neutral proof of an industry-wide effect. [02-c3]

Another account points toward what teams should measure next. A Flywire presentation transcript says the company did not yet have online evaluations and described them as a roadmap goal. The transcript is machine-generated and has not been audio-verified, so treat that passage cautiously; it describes Flywire’s account, not the industry as a whole. [02-c5]

Together, these accounts suggest a practical standard for trusting an agent with more workflow: check the output, then measure the effect on the surrounding work. Start with a bounded, repeatable task. Record task quality, review effort, rework, runtime, and cost, and compare the workflow before and after the agent under the same conditions. Expand its role when quality holds and the combined effort falls. That is an editorial recommendation, not a measured gain in the evidence here. [02-c2] [02-c4] [02-c5]

The excerpts available for several sources are sampled, so they cannot establish what those full sources do or do not report. The evidence therefore supports neither a universal productivity figure nor a verdict on small-team delivery. The next edition could resolve a more useful question: in a small team’s bounded trial, do agent-assisted changes preserve quality while reducing total review, rework, runtime, and cost?

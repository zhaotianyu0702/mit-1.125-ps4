## Narration

You’re deciding whether to let a coding assistant take on more of your team’s work. What would justify that step? A faster run is useful evidence, but it does not answer whether the whole delivery process got faster, whether the result was sound, or whether review and rework erased the gain.

The evidence here points toward a practical answer: experiment actively, but on bounded tasks. Measure whether quality holds and whether total effort falls. Expand uses when both support it. That is an editorial recommendation, not a measured productivity result.

Consider Asana’s browser-agent study. The company says it ran 144 tests across four models on one fixed task: collecting six fields for each of 32 books from a public demo catalog. Its optimized GPT-6.1 Sol workflow averaged about four minutes and an estimated 47 cents in model costs per run. Asana reports it was 76 times cheaper and five times faster than the original production setup on Model B. Every optimized run completed the task with the correct answer. **[02-c1] [02-c2]**

Those results support a clear claim about that task and setup. They do not measure total delivery effort, including the people who set up, review, and maintain a workflow. Nor do they show what happens across varied small-team software work.

Now compare that with ReviewBench, a GitHub code-review benchmark. It evaluates reviewers against pull-request findings, with metrics for different review preferences. GitHub says senior engineers independently relabeled benchmark findings, agreeing with its judgments 96.6 percent of the time. That is a validation measure for the benchmark’s labels; it is not a measurement of production impact by itself. **[02-c4]**

Here’s the cross-source point: the Asana study measures cost, runtime, and correctness on a fixed browser task. ReviewBench measures code-review behavior against a benchmark. They answer different questions, so combining them cannot establish general delivery gains for small teams. To answer that, a team needs evidence from its own workflow and conditions.

The other examples reinforce the need to measure the outcome that matters. GitHub reports that from the second quarter of 2024 to the second quarter of 2026, screened pushes grew 2.84 times, while pushes carrying credentials grew 2.59 times. It found no statistically detectable trend in per-push prevalence across nine complete quarters. That is a reported security trend, not a measure of coding-agent productivity or proof of what caused the trend. **[02-c3]**

A Flywire presentation transcript says the company did not yet have online evaluations and described them as a roadmap goal. The passage is machine-transcribed and has not been audio-verified; it describes Flywire’s account, not the industry as a whole. **[02-c5]**

For a small team, the next step is concrete: choose one repeatable task, define what a correct result means, and record review time, rework, runtime, and costs alongside task quality. Compare the whole workflow, not a single model run. Then expand where quality holds and total effort falls. The supplied studies make that kind of experiment worth pursuing; they do not tell us in advance what gains your team will see.

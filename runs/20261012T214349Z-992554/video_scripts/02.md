# What Coding-Agent Results Actually Measure

**As of: 2026-10-12**  
**Companion video script | Approx. 3–5 minutes | About 520 spoken words**

## Narration

When a coding agent gets cheaper, runs more often, or earns a better benchmark score, what have we actually learned? Those are useful measurements. But none, on its own, tells a small team whether it can ship better software with less total effort.

Consider Asana’s browser-agent study. The company says it ran 144 tests comparing four models and different settings for history, caching, and screenshots. Each run gathered six fields for each of 32 books from a public demo catalog. In the optimized workflow, Asana reports an average runtime of about four minutes and estimated model cost of 47 cents. All 18 runs using the larger history budget returned correct answers. The study also found that history handling affected whether the agent produced an answer at all.

That’s concrete evidence that workflow choices can change cost, speed, and completion on this particular information-extraction task. It is not a coding productivity result.

A different distinction appears in LegalOn’s account of Codex use. LegalOn reports that model selection, feature restrictions, and budget policies reduced estimated daily AI costs by about 65%, while saying development speed was maintained. But the company also says it is still building a pipeline for a metric that links customer value to the AI cost of each feature release. The cost figure is a reported operational measure; customer value per release is not yet measured by that metric.

GitHub’s ReviewBench offers a more code-specific way to ask whether a change helps. GitHub describes a versioned benchmark for AI code review, and reports that offline changes tested with ReviewBench consistently pointed in the same direction as later production results in its experiments. That is a promising evaluation signal: teams can test review changes against a fixed, inspectable setup before relying on them in production. The reported agreement comes from GitHub’s own account, though, not independent replication—and it does not tell us how the benchmark transfers to a small team’s repository.

Now compare those measures with Flywire’s presentation about its Orbit workflow platform. Its presenters report more than 9,700 automated code-review runs in the past 30 days. They also say online evaluations are not yet available, and describe them as work in progress. Run volume tells us that the system was used at scale within that setting. By itself, it does not establish review quality, reduced human review effort, or improved delivery.

Here’s the cross-source insight: operational signals can move in the right direction without answering the team-level question. Asana reports lower cost and faster completion on one browser task; LegalOn reports lower estimated costs while its customer-value measure is still being built; Flywire reports many runs while saying online evaluation is pending. These are different tasks and systems, not numbers we can combine into a productivity estimate. More detail makes a claim easier to inspect, but does not make it independently confirmed or generalizable.

So what would justify trusting an agent with more of a small team’s workflow? Start with representative tasks from your own repository. Check outputs independently, count failures, measure review and correction time, and include total cost. Compare against your existing process, then repeat across enough work to see whether results hold. A code-review benchmark can help evaluate one part of the workflow; it cannot stand in for all of it.

The evidence here documents real task, cost, and evaluation measurements. It does not establish that coding agents improve delivery productivity for small teams. That conclusion needs measurements on their work.

# What Coding-Agent Results Actually Measure

As of: 2026-10-12

A lower model bill, a completed benchmark task, and thousands of automated runs can all be real measurements. None alone tells a small team whether an assistant helped ship better software with less total effort. The useful connection across recent reports is that each measures a different link in the delivery chain—and the links between them remain largely unmeasured. [02-c1] [02-c2] [02-c4] [02-c5]

## Operational gains are not delivery gains

[Asana’s report](https://openai.com/index/asana-browser-agent) describes a 144-run study comparing four models and variations in history, caching, and screenshot handling. For a fixed browser task—extracting six fields for each of 32 books—the company says its optimized GPT-6.1 Sol workflow averaged about four minutes and $0.47 in estimated model cost, and all 18 runs using a larger history budget returned correct answers. The result is useful evidence that workflow configuration can change cost, runtime, and task completion on this particular information-extraction task. It is not a coding-agent productivity result. [02-c1]

That distinction matters because software delivery has additional costs and failure points. A coding agent may produce a patch quickly, yet leave a person to discover a regression, repair an incomplete change, or review noisy findings. Those are questions for measurement, not conclusions we can draw from Asana’s browser experiment. The publisher presents the work as a customer benefit and a demonstration of its tools; the reported task results are concrete, but their scope remains narrow. [02-c1]

## Cost savings leave value open

[LegalOn’s account](https://openai.com/index/legalon-halves-codex-costs) reports that model selection, feature restrictions, and budgets tailored to different business areas reduced estimated daily AI costs by about 65%. It also says development speed was maintained. But the company explicitly says it is still building the pipeline for a metric that connects each feature release’s customer value with the AI cost invested in it. [02-c2]

That admission connects a cost report to the harder question: what did the spending produce? Lower estimated usage costs may help manage a budget, but they do not by themselves show that releases improved customer outcomes, reduced engineering effort, or justified review time. LegalOn’s account is a company case study, and its commercial interest is to describe the benefits of its AI-enabled development approach. Its cost figure is a reported estimate; the customer-value measure is a planned evaluation, not a result. [02-c2]

## Evaluation is a bridge, not the destination

[GitHub’s ReviewBench post](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/reviewbench-an-open-benchmark-for-ai-code-review/) offers a more directly relevant method: a versioned benchmark for code review. GitHub says it versions the dataset, judge, and matcher, and reports that offline changes evaluated before A/B tests have consistently pointed in the same direction as later production results. That is a potentially useful signal for deciding which code-review changes merit a production experiment. [02-c3]

There are two boundaries. First, ReviewBench measures code-review behavior, not whether coding agents improve end-to-end delivery for small teams. Second, the reported offline-to-production alignment comes from GitHub’s own account of its experiments. The post describes internal validation and invites others to use the benchmark, but the evidence supplied here does not include independent replication. A benchmark can make comparisons more reproducible without proving that its results transfer to a different repository, workflow, or team. [02-c3]

Flywire’s [presentation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAUyRDOFyHU&t=944s) adds a useful operational contrast. Its presenters report more than 9,700 automated code-review runs in 30 days, while also saying online evaluations are not yet available ([evaluation discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAUyRDOFyHU&t=907s)). Run volume shows that a workflow is being used; it does not establish that reviews catch important defects, reduce human review burden, or improve delivery. The presenters describe an enterprise platform and its controls, and note that their container limitations can prevent native builds or unit tests for some agents. That context limits what a high run count can tell us about output quality. [02-c4] [02-c5]

Taken together, the reports show why operational detail is valuable but insufficient. Asana measures one task’s completion, time, and estimated model cost; LegalOn reports cost control while developing a release-value measure; Flywire reports workflow volume while its online evaluation remains in progress. These quantities cannot be combined into a shared productivity estimate. The sources also differ in task, system, and scale, and are chiefly vendor or platform accounts. Their reported figures are evidence about the conditions they describe, not independent confirmation of team-level gains. [02-c5]

For a small team, a credible trial should follow a representative task through the whole workflow: independently check the artifact, record failures and rework, count human review time, and include total cost. Compare against the team’s usual process over enough similar tasks to see variation. That checklist is an interpretation of what these reports leave unresolved, not a result already demonstrated by them. A bounded task may still benefit from automation; the evidence here does not establish that outcome for any particular team. [02-c5]

Which representative repository task will the next edition measure, and will its verified quality, repair and review effort, failure rate, and total cost show a net delivery gain?
